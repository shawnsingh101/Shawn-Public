Disasters and other emergencies can happen at any time, anywhere, and vary in magnitude. Older adults and people with disabilities often have unique needs during a crisis. For example, approximately half of those 65 and older have two or more chronic health problems (such as heart disease, diabetes, or Alzheimer’s disease), which can make them more vulnerable during periods of time without food, water, shelter, or adequate rest. People with disabilities also may have underlying health conditions that increase their risks during a crisis or may require services and supports that are often scarce during a crisis event. 
That’s why planning to meet the needs of older adults and people with disabilities — at the community and individual level — is so important. 
We’ve compiled some resources and tools to help! You can browse by topic below, or visit Ready.gov to find a wide variety of resources for preparedness planning, such as several social media toolkits, including one for National Preparedness Month. The HHS Administration for Strategic Preparedness & Response also has information and resources that may be useful.

Emergency Preparedness for Older Adults and People with Disabilities 
Everyone should prepare for potential crises and plan how to respond. Providers of long-term services and supports must train staff on emergency actions, including evacuation, lockdown, and sheltering in place. Community leaders and first responders must be prepared to support the health and safety of older adults and people with disabilities, engage them in emergency planning, and — to the maximum extent possible — provide services and supports in shelters.
Ensuring the well-being of older adults and people with disabilities during a crisis requires a whole community approach to preparedness.
 Individual Preparedness: Best Practices and Resources 
Older adults, people with disabilities, and caregivers can take steps to prepare for emergencies. Most older adults and people with disabilities live in the community. Many live alone and require long-term services and supports to continue living independently in the community. Without appropriate contingency plans, individuals with mobility limitations, those who rely on battery- or electrically powered medical devices or durable medical equipment, and those with other functional needs may be at greater risk during a crisis. However, by assessing each person’s unique needs, person-centered plans can be developed to accommodate those needs and maximize independence.
At a minimum, everyone (with the assistance of their caregiver, if necessary) should create an emergency kit. This should include medication, food, water, batteries or chargers, and any supplies that pets or service animals may need. It should also include the locations of nearby shelters.
ACL has created two tools that are also important parts of an emergency kit. This Medical Information Card captures important information that could be needed by emergency responders and medical personnel during a crisis. The Communication Assistance Card is a tool to help first responders assist a person whose disability may make it difficult for them to communicate their needs.
Individuals should also talk to friends, family, and neighbors to create a support network that can help with communication, transportation, and essential care during periods of time when other community-based services and supports are unavailable.

 Preparedness for Community-Based Organizations and Providers of Healthcare Services: Best Practices and Resources 
Organizations that provide long-term services and supports to older adults and people with disabilities (including home health providers, hospitals, nursing homes, and other service providers) face unique obstacles during a crisis. They are frequently called on to provide leadership before, during, and after a disaster. They must also develop emergency plans to protect their service recipients and staff during an emergency. Those plans must also include well-tested, accessible transportation procedures to support safe evacuations when necessary.
All staff members must know their roles and responsibilities under the service provider’s emergency response plans, especially during an evacuation. Emergency management plans should be reviewed and tested regularly to identify gaps, build in redundancies, and develop strategies to address them. 
Many types of providers are required to develop emergency response plans to meet licensure or certification requirements. In addition to these requirements, collaboration with other agencies and participation in community-wide drills and exercises can help ensure that the service provider’s emergency plans are integrated with local, regional, and state response efforts. It is also important to ensure that backup power generators are available and operational, evacuation transportation plans are in place, and procedures are established for communicating with the relatives of care recipients during an emergency.

 Community Preparedness: Best Practices and Resources 
Older adults and people with disabilities are frequently overlooked during emergency preparedness, response, and recovery efforts. Emergency management efforts must include older adults and people with disabilities of all ages — and their caregivers — in every aspect of community emergency planning, response, and recovery. They should also be included in the development of emergency plans through public meetings. Meetings should be held close to public transportation, scheduled when public transportation is available, and offer virtual attendance options. Shelters should be assessed for accessibility, and supplies should be readily available to meet the needs of people with disabilities and older adults.
To effectively integrate older adults and people with disabilities into emergency preparedness, response, and recovery efforts, officials must take a whole-community approach when considering their needs during a crisis. Key partners include hospitals, local providers of long-term services and supports, area agencies on aging, centers for independent living, protection & advocacy agencies, developmental disabilities councils, long-term care ombudsman programs, and aging and disability resource centers. These organizations should be included in the coordination, planning, and execution of joint exercises involving state and local law enforcement, first responders, and other community partners to help ensure the needs of all community members are considered and protected during emergencies.
